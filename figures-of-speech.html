1 Start Here: Figures of Speech in TNPSC

Begin here. This section explains what a figure of speech is, what the 2022 syllabus names, which 25 poems it lists, how questions are framed and how to finish these notes in one week.

1.1 What is a figure of speech?

A figure of speech is a special way of using words to make an idea more vivid, more musical or more forceful. Literal language says exactly what it means. Figurative language compares, repeats, plays with sound or exaggerates, so that the reader sees, hears or feels more.

Figures come in two broad kinds:

  • Figures of meaning — the words mean more than they say: Simile, Metaphor, Personification, Hyperbole.
  • Figures of sound and pattern — the sound or the arrangement of the words is special: Alliteration, Onomatopoeia, Anaphora, Repetition.

Rhyme scheme and rhyming words are really sound patterns. The syllabus lists them with the figures of speech, so these notes treat them as figures too (see 3.8 and 3.9).

Tamil-medium students already know two of them from Tamil grammar: uvamai ani is the Simile and uruvaga ani is the Metaphor.

Why poets use them

  • To paint a picture. Like a child at play. lets you see the young river as a playful child.
  • To make music. Repeated sounds and rhymes make lines pleasant to hear and easy to remember.
  • To stress an idea. And miles to go before I sleep comes twice, to stress how much work is still left.
  • To show strong feeling in a few words: "Oh!", "Alas!".
  • To make big ideas easy to grasp. Anger becomes a growing tree (5.8), life becomes a journey (5.1) and a river becomes a human life (5.11).

Literal or figurative?

Literal sentenceFigurative sentenceFigure
Chennai in May is very hot.Chennai in May is as hot as an oven.Simile (3.2) — as ... as compares the city with an oven

Rows 1 and 2 carry the same idea. With "as ... as" it is a simile; with only "is" and no comparing word, it is a metaphor. Learn this difference first; Section 6 explains more such pairs.

Try two

QC1. Identify the figure of speech: Fly as thick as driving rain; (PYQ-style)

  1. Alliteration
  2. Simile
  3. Onomatopoeia
  4. Anaphora
Answer

Answer: (b) - Simile: "as thick as" compares the rushing sights with heavy rain, two unlike things. Eliminate the rest: no sound-word (not c), no repeated first sound (not a), and no word repeated at the start of lines or clauses (not d).

  2. abab
  3. abcb
  4. abba
Answer

Answer: (b) - mine = a, pit = b, design rhymes with mine = a, fit rhymes with pit = b: abab.

Telling family members apart

  • Comparison: "like / as" = Simile; "is" = Metaphor; a thing acting like a human = Personification; a thing or an absent person spoken TO = Apostrophe.
  • Sound: same first consonant sound = Alliteration; same vowel sound = Assonance; same consonant sound inside or at the end = Consonance; a word that copies a noise = Onomatopoeia.
  • Repetition and order: same opening word(s), the rest of the line changing = Anaphora; same word(s) at the END = Epistrophe; a line closing every stanza = Refrain; a whole line said again, or a word repeated ANYWHERE = Repetition.
  • Contrast: two opposite WORDS side by side = Oxymoron; two opposite IDEAS in balance = Antithesis; a whole statement that seems absurd but is true = Paradox.
  • Exaggeration and understatement: bigger = Hyperbole; smaller, often with "not" = Litotes; softer = Euphemism.

3.1 Alliteration IN SYLLABUS Sound

MeaningThe same first sound is repeated in words that stand close together.
Exam definitionAlliteration is the repetition of the same beginning consonant SOUND in two or more nearby words of a line or phrase. It depends on SOUND, not on spelling.
Key / clue wordsNo fixed clue word — the clue is a repeated first sound: f ... f k = c = q f = ph s ≠ sh say it aloud
How to spot it1. Say the line aloud (or in your mind). 2. Listen to the FIRST sound of each important word — ignore the spelling. 3. Two or more nearby words with the same first consonant sound = alliteration. Small words (a, the, of, and) may come in between.
Memory trickALL-ITERATION: ALL the words start with the same SOUND — say it aloud. Trust your EARS, not your EYES.

Easy examples

  • Amma made dosa, dal and delicious chutney. d - d - d
  • The big blue bus braked at the busy bus stand. b sound
  • Kumar kept cool with coconut water. k sound: K and C
  • Father's phone fell on the floor. f sound: F and PH
  • Seven silly sparrows sat on the Marina sand. s sound
  • Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers. (classic tongue twister) p sound

MUST REMEMBER: Alliteration gives a line RHYTHM and MUSIC and makes it EASY TO REMEMBER — that is why proverbs (Practice makes perfect; Look before you leap; Waste not, want not; Time and tide wait for none), tongue twisters, slogans and poems use it. Vowel-sound alliteration exists but is rare; in the exam, alliteration almost always means a repeated CONSONANT sound. Repeated vowel sounds are Assonance (4.5); repeated consonants inside or at the end of words are Consonance (4.6).

From the syllabus poems

My heart will keep the courage of the quest,
Life · Henry Van Dyke
Alliteration (k sound) — keep, courage, quest: three different letters (k, c, q) but ONE sound. The best proof that alliteration is about sound, not letters.
Faster than fairies, faster than witches,
From a Railway Carriage · Robert Louis Stevenson
Alliteration (f) — the quick f sounds copy the speed of the train. The line is ALSO a simile (than) and hyperbole; if the options clash, alliteration is the safest single answer.
To watch his woods fill up with snow.
Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening · Robert Frost
Alliteration (w) — watch, woods, with. The same stanza has His house is in the village though; (h). But the FIRST line of the poem is a trap — see below.
And all I ask is a tall ship and a star to steer her by;
Sea Fever · John Masefield
Alliteration (st) — star, steer. The poem is full of it: And the wheel's kick and the wind's song and the white sail's shaking, (w).
And I sunned it with smiles,
A Poison Tree · William Blake
Alliteration (s) — sunned, smiles. The same line is part of the extended metaphor of anger as a tree (see 3.3).
On you rush through rough and smooth;
The River · Caroline Ann Bowles
Alliteration (r) — rush, rough: the rolling r suits the rushing young river. Also Down you dash into the sea, (d).
And though he twist and twirl, and tack,
A Tragic Story · William Makepeace Thackeray
Alliteration (t) — twist, twirl, tack. "Though" begins with a th sound, so it is NOT part of the pattern.
A daring deed in a moment's flash;
Courage · Edgar Albert Guest
Alliteration (d) — daring, deed.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Whose woods these are I think I know. is NOT alliteration. "Whose" begins with an h sound (hooz) and "woods" with a w sound — similar letters, different sounds. This famous line is Inversion (4.20). The real alliteration in this poem is To watch his woods fill up with snow. (w).

DO NOT CONFUSE: s is not sh. In For simple sheep; and such are daffodils and but short and sweet, the two words begin with different sounds (s / sh), so the pairs are not true alliteration. Also, the same WORD said again (River, river, little river!) is Repetition (3.7); alliteration needs DIFFERENT words with the same first sound.

3.2 Simile IN SYLLABUS Comparison

MeaningTwo different things are compared openly, using a comparing word such as "like" or "as".
Exam definitionA simile is an explicit (open, direct) comparison between two UNLIKE things that share a quality, made with a comparing word: like, as, as ... as, so ... as, as if, as though, or than.
Key / clue wordslike as as ... as so ... as as if as though than
How to spot it1. Find the comparing word. 2. Check that two UNLIKE things are compared (charging animals and troops, a friend and a flower). 3. Find the shared quality (speed, care). All three present = simile.
Memory trickSimile Says "like"; Metaphor Means "is". SIMILE sounds like SIMILAR — it openly says one thing is similar to another.

Easy examples

  • Chennai in May is as hot as an oven. as ... as
  • The Marina sand shines like gold in the evening sun. like
  • The auto-rickshaw zipped through the traffic as if it had wings. as if
  • After the first rain, the paddy fields looked as though someone had spread a green carpet. as though
  • In the kabaddi match, Selvi ran faster than a deer. than
  • "Twinkle, twinkle, little star … Like a diamond in the sky." (classic nursery rhyme) like

3.3 Metaphor IN SYLLABUS Comparison

MeaningOne thing is said to BE another thing, without "like" or "as".
Exam definitionA metaphor is an implied (hidden) comparison between two unlike things: one thing is spoken of as if it were the other, without a comparing word. It also covers a single word used in a non-literal (figurative) sense, such as leperous hide or the road's last turn.
Key / clue wordsis / are / was X of Y no like / as a word used non-literally
How to spot it1. Something is compared, but there is NO like / as / than. 2. Read it literally — it is impossible (a woman cannot really be a lioness). 3. Ask "in what way are they alike?" (brave, fierce). That shared quality is the hidden comparison.
Memory trickSimile Says "like"; Metaphor Means "is". Metaphor = Simile minus "like".

Easy examples

  • Chennai in May is a furnace. is (no like)
  • The classroom was a fish market before the teacher came. was
  • Our headmaster has a heart of gold. heart of gold
  • My grandfather is a walking encyclopedia. is
  • The teacher's sharp words stung me. stung (a verb)
  • "All the world's a stage." (Shakespeare, As You Like It) 's = is

3.7 Repetition IN SYLLABUS Repetition and order

MeaningA word, a phrase or a whole line is used again to stress an idea or a feeling.
Exam definitionRepetition is the deliberate use of the same word, phrase or line more than once - anywhere in a line, stanza or poem - for emphasis, rhythm and feeling. Anaphora (3.6), epistrophe (4.21) and refrain (4.22) are special kinds of repetition, named by WHERE the repeated words stand.
Key / clue wordsNo clue word - look for the SAME words coming back: no, no never, never round and round my wrath, my wrath we ants never the battle is today's woman comet in full flight behind him Remember
How to spot it1. Find a word, phrase or line that comes back. 2. Check WHERE it comes back: start of lines = anaphora; end of lines = epistrophe; end of every stanza = refrain; anywhere else, or the whole line again = repetition. 3. If no special name fits, or none is offered, answer "Repetition" - all of them are repetition.
Memory trickAmma calls, "Come, come, come home!" - she repeats because it is IMPORTANT. Poets repeat for the same reason: EMPHASIS. Then let the POSITION give the name: start = anaphora, end = epistrophe, end of every stanza = refrain, anywhere or whole line = repetition.

Easy examples

  • Hurry, hurry, the school bus is at the gate! hurry, hurry - a word
  • It rained and rained and rained all night in Chennai. rained - a word
  • Practice, practice, practice - that is the secret of every good cricketer. practice - a word
  • Slowly and steadily, slowly and steadily, the tortoise won the race. slowly and steadily - a phrase
  • Happy birthday to you, happy birthday to you ... a whole line sung again
  • Water, water, every where (Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner) water, water - a word

DO NOT CONFUSE: (1) Repetition vs anaphora vs refrain - all three repeat words; the POSITION decides the name: start of lines = anaphora (3.6), end of every stanza = refrain (4.22), anywhere else or a whole line = repetition. 3.8 Rhyme Scheme IN SYLLABUS Sound

MeaningThe PATTERN of rhyming sounds at the ends of lines, written with small letters: a, b, c ...
Exam definitionRhyme scheme is the ordered pattern of end rhymes in a stanza or poem, marked with small letters: lines that end with the same sound get the same letter, and each new end-sound gets the next letter of the alphabet.
Key / clue wordsIn the question: rhyme scheme pattern of rhyme first stanza aabb abab abcb aaba. In the poem: ONLY the last word of each line.
How to spot it1. Underline the last word of every line. 2. Say the end sounds aloud - ears, not eyes. 3. Letter them: first sound = a; a sound heard before = its old letter; a new sound = the next letter.
Memory trickNew end-sound = new letter. Same sound, same letter. Like seats on a bus: each NEW passenger (sound) gets the next seat letter; a passenger who comes back sits in his OLD seat.

Easy examples

  • Twinkle, twinkle, little star, / How I wonder what you are! star - are = aa (a couplet)
  • I eat my idli hot, / I finish all I've got; / Then off to school I run, / To learn and play in the sun. hot - got, run - sun = aabb
  • The monsoon clouds are grey, / The farmers smile with cheer, / The rain has come to stay, / Good harvest for the year. grey - stay, cheer - year = abab
  • My mother wakes at five, / She lights the kitchen fire, / She cooks and does not tire, / The whole house comes alive. five - alive outside, fire - tire inside = abba
  • On Marina Beach at evening / The waves come rolling in; / The kites fly high above us, / The sea wind on my skin. only in - skin rhyme = abcb
  • The bus is late. / I stand at the stop / and count the autos / turning at the signal. no end rhyme = free verse: no rhyme scheme

The 5-step method

  1. Find the line-end words. Write the last word of every line in a column. Ignore commas, full stops and quotation marks.
  2. Say the end sounds aloud. Take the last stressed vowel and everything after it: know → -oh, here → -eer, dancing → -ancing. Trust your EAR, not the spelling: though also ends in -oh.
  3. First sound = a. The first line always gets a.
  4. New sound = next letter; same sound = same letter. Compare each line with the sounds already used. A sound heard before takes its OLD letter; a new sound takes the NEXT unused letter (b, c, d ...).
  5. Write stanza by stanza. Leave a space between stanzas (aaba bbcb ...). A sound that comes back in a later stanza keeps its old letter.

Notation rules

Use small letters, written together, with a space between stanzas.aabb ccdd - not AABB, not 1122

3.9 Rhyming Words IN SYLLABUS Sound

MeaningWords whose END sounds are the same: night - bright, friend - end.
Exam definitionRhyming words are words that share the same final sound - the last stressed vowel and every sound after it - while their opening sounds differ. In poems they usually stand at the ends of lines. Near (slant) rhymes such as arms - harm and smooth - youth are accepted in textbook answers.
Key / clue wordsIn the question: rhyming words rhymes with pick out the rhyming pair words that rhyme. In the poem: the line-end words of a couplet (aabb) or of lines 2 and 4 (abcb).
How to spot it1. Take the last word of each line. 2. Cut off the opening sound and compare the TAILS: n-ight / br-ight. 3. Tails that sound the same = rhyme. Judge by SOUND, not spelling (foe - grow rhyme; home - come do not).
Memory trickSame TAIL, different HEAD. n-IGHT, br-IGHT, fl-IGHT: the heads change, the tail stays. A tail that only LOOKS the same (h-OME, c-OME) does not count.

Easy examples

  • cat - hat - mat - rat tail -at
  • rain - train - plain - chain tail -ain
  • light - bright - kite - height one sound, three spellings: -ight, -ite, -eight
  • blue - shoe - through - too one -oo sound, four spellings
  • dancing - glancing; singing - ringing two-syllable (double) rhymes
  • home - come; love - move NOT true rhymes - they only LOOK alike (eye rhymes)

How to pick rhyming words

  1. List the line-end words, stanza by stanza.
  2. Cut off the head - the opening consonant sound(s): fr-iend / end; f-oe / gr-ow.
  3. Compare the tails - the final vowel sound plus the consonant(s) after it must SOUND the same: -end / -end; -oh / -oh.
  4. Ignore spelling: foe - grow, though - snow, stair - there and far - are all rhyme.
  5. Prefer line-end pairs. In an MCQ the correct pair is usually the two ends of one couplet (aabb) or lines 2 and 4 (abcb). A pair taken from INSIDE a line is usually the trap.

From the syllabus poems

I was angry with my foe: I told it not, my wrath did grow.
A Poison Tree · William Blake
foe - grow — different spellings (-oe, -ow), the SAME -oh sound: a true rhyme that closes a couplet (aabb).
Faster than fairies, faster than witches, Bridges and houses, hedges and ditches;
From a Railway Carriage · Robert Louis Stevenson
witches - ditches — a two-syllable rhyme (-itches). The whole poem runs in such quick couplets (aabb), like the rattle of the train.
The woods are lovely, dark and deep, But I have promises to keep,
Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening · Robert Frost
deep - keep (and sleep) — the d rhyme that fills the last stanza (dddd, see 3.8).
And the wheel's kick and the wind's song and the white sail's shaking, And a grey mist on the sea's face, and a grey dawn breaking.
Sea Fever · John Masefield
shaking - breaking — a two-syllable rhyme (-aking) at the ends of a couplet. Words such as grey ... face inside the line share only a vowel sound - that is assonance (4.5), not rhyme.
It's all very well to have courage and skill And it's fine to be counted a star, But the single deed with its touch of thrill Doesn't tell the man you are;

skill - thrill (lines 1 and 3) and star - are (lines 2 and 4) — alternate rhyme, abab. star and are look different but sound alike.

dead - read — here read is the PAST form, said "red", so this is a true rhyme. Say the word the way the poem means it.
you would hold me in your arms. keeping me safe from harm.

arms - harm (lines 2 and 4) — a slant (near) rhyme: the extra s does not stop it counting. The poem's scheme is abcb, so lines 2 and 4 are where the rhymes are.

Traps to avoid

TrapSyllabus exampleWhat to do
1. Eye rhyme - looks alike, sounds differentBegan to complain when he found that, at home, / His cupboard was empty, and winter was come. (home - come)home = "hohm", come = "kum": not a true rhyme. The poet uses it as a couplet, so the scheme stays aabb; but if asked for a TRUE rhyming pair, pick one that really sounds alike (sing - spring).
2. The same word repeatedThe brave man never is freed of it. / He has it when there is no need of it. (it - it)A repeated word is only an identical rhyme - the real echo here is freed - need. The same goes for sleep - sleep (Stopping by Woods), him - him (A Tragic Story's refrain) and beliefs - belief (I am Every Woman): one letter in the scheme, but not the answer when a true pair is offered.
3. Internal chime - an echo INSIDE a line (sometimes called internal rhyme)Of scorching and chokingThe two words stand in ONE line, so they are not a pair of line-end words, and only their -ing endings chime (scorch / choke differ). Exam keys treat the repeated ch sound as consonance (4.6). On Killing a Tree is free verse with NO end rhymes, so these are not its rhyming words. A cleaner internal rhyme is thaw - saw in She knows how to thaw you, saw you - so beware! - but it is still not a line-end pair.
4. Slant rhyme - ACCEPTEDarms - harm (Special Hero); smooth - youth (The River)Near rhymes count in textbook answers. Do not reject a pair just because one sound differs slightly.

4 More Figures You Must Know

The 2022 revised TNPSC General English syllabus names nine figures of speech (Section 3) and ends the list with "etc." So other figures of speech can also be asked. This section gives you 27 more figures in short cards: meaning, exam definition, clue words, how to spot it, a memory trick, easy examples, examples from the 25 syllabus poems, one trap and a quick check. Apostrophe, Allusion, Oxymoron and Ellipsis (4.1–4.4) were named in the earlier syllabus and are still worth knowing — learn them first. Then revise the cards marked HIGH-FREQUENCY, and finally the rest.

EXAM TIP: One line can carry two or three figures at once. Read what the question points to — a word, a sound, a comparison or a hidden meaning — and choose the figure for that part. For every figure in each poem, see Section 5; for look-alike pairs, see Section 6.

4.1 Apostrophe ALSO ASKED Comparison HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningSpeaking directly to someone or something that cannot answer — a dead or absent person, a thing or an idea. Say: uh-POS-truh-fee. (named in the earlier syllabus)
Exam definitionApostrophe is directly addressing someone absent or dead, or a thing or idea, as if it could hear. Addressing a listener who is present (a doctor in the room) is NOT apostrophe.
Key / clue wordsO ... Oh ... a name + comma at the start you / thou / thy (said to a thing) !
How to spot it1. Is the speaker talking TO someone or something ("you", "O ...")? 2. Can that listener really hear and reply? 3. A thing, an idea, the dead or someone far away → apostrophe. It usually comes with personification.
Memory trickPERSONification turns a thing into a PERSON. APOSTROPHE POSTs a message to someone who cannot reply.

Easy examples

  • "Twinkle, twinkle, little star, / How I wonder what you are!" speaks to a star
  • "Milton! thou shouldst be living at this hour." (Wordsworth) speaks to Milton, a poet long dead
  • "Rain, rain, go away; come again another day." speaks to the rain

From the syllabus poems

River, river, little river! Bright you sparkle on your way;
The River · Caroline Ann Bowles
Apostrophe + Personification — the poet calls out to the river and says "you" to it as if it could hear. Making it sparkle and travel also gives it life (see 3.4).
And, O weary, wandering brother, if contentment you would win, Come you back unto the fireside and be comrade with your kin.
The Stick-together Families · Edgar Albert Guest
Apostrophe — "O" + a direct call to an absent brother who has wandered far from home.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Apostrophe = talking TO the thing (the poet says "you" to the river). Personification = only giving the thing human actions or feelings (3.4). The two often come together. In My Computer Needs a Break the child asks Doctor, do you think my tired computer wants a holiday? — the doctor is present in the room, so this is NOT apostrophe. And this figure has nothing to do with the punctuation mark (') of the same name.

Quick check

QC1. Identify the figure of speech: River, river, little river! (PYQ-style)

  1. Simile
  2. Apostrophe
  3. Metaphor
  4. Onomatopoeia
Answer

Answer: (b) - the poet speaks directly to the river as if it could hear.

4.2 Allusion ALSO ASKED Association and reference HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningA quick hint to a famous person, place, event or story that the writer expects you to know. (named in the earlier syllabus)
Exam definitionAllusion is a brief reference to a well-known person, place, event, story or text (the Bible, the epics, history, literature), given without explanation.
Key / clue wordsFamous names from epics, myths, history and books: Karna Kumbhakarna Pandora's box Herculean task Achilles' heel Eden / the apple
How to spot it1. Find a famous name or story that the writer does not explain. 2. Ask: "Do I need outside knowledge (an epic, a myth, history, a famous book) to get the full meaning?" Yes → allusion.
Memory trickALLusion points to a story ALL of us know — one word opens a whole story.

Easy examples

  • He is a Karna in generosity. Karna, the Mahabharata
  • On holidays my cousin becomes a Kumbhakarna — he sleeps till noon. Kumbhakarna, the Ramayana
  • Raising old quarrels at a wedding opens a Pandora's box. Greek myth

From the syllabus poems

And it grew both day and night, Till it bore an apple bright.
A Poison Tree · William Blake
Allusion — the shining apple recalls the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden (the Bible): something tempting that brings death.
Speak gently! - He who gave his life To bend man's stubborn will, When elements were in fierce strife, Said to them, 'Peace, be still.'
Your Space · David Bates
Allusion — to the Bible story in which Jesus calms a stormy sea with the words "Peace, be still".

DO NOT CONFUSE: ALLusion (a reference to a known story) ≠ ILLusion (a false picture or belief). "As generous as Karna" is a simile (as ... as) that also carries an allusion (Karna). If the question asks about the famous name, answer allusion; if it asks about the comparing words, answer simile.

Quick check

QC2. Identify the figure of speech: Till it bore an apple bright. (PYQ-style)

  1. Simile
  2. Oxymoron
  3. Allusion
  4. Hyperbole
Answer

Answer: (c) - the apple refers to the forbidden fruit of the Garden of Eden.

4.3 Oxymoron ALSO ASKED Contrast HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningTwo words with opposite meanings placed together, usually an adjective + a noun. (named in the earlier syllabus)
Exam definitionOxymoron is a figure in which two contradictory words are placed side by side ("empty joy", "deafening silence").
Key / clue wordsdeafening silence open secret bitter-sweet living death cruel kindness empty joy
How to spot it1. Look for a two-word pair. 2. Check if the two words fight each other (silence cannot be deafening). 3. Two clashing words side by side → oxymoron. A whole statement that sounds impossible → paradox (4.11).
Memory trickOXY (sharp) + MORON (dull): the word oxymoron is itself an oxymoron — it is Greek for "sharp-dull".

Easy examples

  • When the results were read out, there was a deafening silence in the hall. deafening + silence
  • It is an open secret that Ravi loves ghee roast. open + secret
  • Leaving school gave us bitter-sweet memories. bitter + sweet

From the syllabus poems

But it's bitterness they harvest, and it's empty joy they find,
The Stick-together Families · Edgar Albert Guest
Oxymoron — joy should fill the heart, but this joy is empty: two opposite ideas sit side by side. (Reaping a harvest of bitterness, in the same line, is a metaphor.)

DO NOT CONFUSE: Oxymoron = two opposite WORDS joined ("empty joy"). Paradox = a whole STATEMENT that sounds impossible but is true ("The child is father of the man", 4.11). Antithesis = two opposite IDEAS in balanced parts ("To err is human, to forgive divine", 4.12).

Quick check

QC3. Identify the figure of speech in the phrase "empty joy": and it's empty joy they find, (PYQ-style)

  1. Oxymoron
  2. Paradox
  3. Pun
  4. Litotes
Answer

Answer: (a) - two opposite words, "empty" and "joy", are placed side by side.

4.4 Ellipsis ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningLeaving out words that the reader can easily fill in; also the three dots (...) that show something is left out. (named in the earlier syllabus)
Exam definitionEllipsis is the leaving out of words that the reader can supply from the context; the name is also used for the mark "..." showing that words have been left out.
Key / clue words... (three dots) a missing verb "Not a ..." phrases one-word answers
How to spot it1. The line feels incomplete, but the meaning is still clear. 2. Supply the missing words ("there was", "could he see") — if they fit easily, it is ellipsis. 3. Three dots in the text = the punctuation mark called ellipsis.
Memory trickELLIPSIS = words LEFT OUT, meaning LEFT IN. The three dots say "you know the rest".

Easy examples

  • Some students came by bus, others by train. "came" left out
  • "Who broke the glass?" "Ravi." = Ravi broke the glass
  • I wanted to tell you ... never mind. the mark ...

From the syllabus poems

Not a crumb to be found On the snow-covered ground; Not a flower could he see, Not a leaf on a tree.
The Ant and the Cricket · adapted from Aesop's fables
Ellipsis — "There was" is left out before the first line, and "could he see" is left out in the last line. (The repeated "Not a" at the start is anaphora, 3.6.)
One bit of courage For the darkening sky;
Making Life Worth While · George Eliot
Ellipsis — the verb "gets" from an earlier line is understood: every soul gets one bit of courage.
Love her, respect her, keep her near...
I am Every Woman · Rakhi Nariani Shirke
Ellipsis (the mark) — the poem's own three dots leave the thought open for the reader to complete.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Ellipsis = any words left out that the reader supplies. Asyndeton = ONLY the joining words (and, or) left out of a list: "I came, I saw, I conquered" (4.23). The mark ellipsis (...) shows missing words; a dash (-) shows a break or an added thought.

Quick check

QC4. In Not a leaf on a tree. the words "could he see" are understood but not written. Identify the figure of speech. (PYQ-style)

  1. Inversion
  2. Polysyndeton
  3. Hyperbole
  4. Ellipsis
Answer

Answer: (d) - words the reader can supply are left out.

4.5 Assonance ALSO ASKED Sound

MeaningThe same vowel sound repeated in words that are close together.
Exam definitionAssonance is the repetition of vowel sounds in nearby words (the consonants around the vowels may be different).
Key / clue wordsRepeated vowel sounds: long a: grey, face, breaking long e: sweep, easy long i: fly, high, sky long o: go, slow, road
How to spot it1. Read the line aloud. 2. Ignore the first letters; listen to the vowel sound in the middle of the words. 3. The same vowel SOUND again and again (even with different spellings: grey / face / break) → assonance.
Memory trickASSONANCE = vowels (A-E-I-O-U); CONSONANCE = consonants.

Easy examples

  • The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain. long a
  • I like to fly my kite high in the sky. long i
  • Go slow on the old road. long o

From the syllabus poems

And a grey mist on the sea's face, and a grey dawn breaking.
Sea Fever · John Masefield
Assonance — the long "a" sound repeats in grey, face, grey and breaking.
Full of sweet dreams, and health, and quiet breathing.
A Thing of Beauty · John Keats
Assonance — the long "e" sound repeats in sweet, dreams and breathing; it makes the line soft and restful.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Assonance = a repeated VOWEL sound anywhere in nearby words (grey - face). Alliteration = a repeated FIRST consonant sound (3.1). Rhyme = matching END sounds, usually at line ends (3.9). In "rain - Spain - plain" the long "a" is assonance, and the whole "-ain" ending is rhyme.

Quick check

QC5. The repeated long "e" sound (sweep - easy) in The only other sound's the sweep / Of easy wind and downy flake. is an example of (PYQ-style)

  1. Alliteration
  2. Consonance
  3. Assonance
  4. Onomatopoeia
Answer

Answer: (c) - a vowel sound, long "e", is repeated in nearby words.

4.6 Consonance ALSO ASKED Sound

MeaningThe same consonant sound repeated in nearby words, mostly in the middle or at the end of the words.
Exam definitionConsonance is the repetition of consonant sounds, usually inside or at the end of nearby words, with different vowels.
Key / clue wordsRepeated middle / end consonants: k: struck, streak, luck ll: all, well dg / tch: hedges, ditches ch: scorching, choking
How to spot it1. Ignore the first letters. 2. Listen to the consonant sounds inside and at the end of the words. 3. The same consonant sound repeated close together → consonance.
Memory trickASSONANCE = vowels (A-E-I-O-U); CONSONANCE = consonants. Consonance hides in the middle and at the END of words.

Easy examples

  • He struck a streak of bad luck. k sound: struck, streak, luck
  • All's well that ends well. l sound: all's, well, well
  • Odds and ends lay on the table. d sound: odds, and, ends

From the syllabus poems

Of scorching and choking
On Killing a Tree · Gieve Patel
Consonance — the "ch" sound repeats in scorching and choking; the two -ing endings add to the harsh sound of the tree's slow death.
Bridges and houses, hedges and ditches;
From a Railway Carriage · Robert Louis Stevenson
Consonance — the "j" sound (dg) repeats inside bridges and hedges, ditches answers it with the close "ch" sound (tch), and all four nouns end in the same -es sound, like the steady clatter of the train.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Consonance = a consonant sound repeated INSIDE or at the END of words (struck - luck). Alliteration = a consonant sound repeated at the START of words (3.1). Some books call alliteration a special kind of consonance; if both are options and the sound is at the start of the words, choose alliteration.

Quick check

QC6. The repeated "dg" and "tch" sounds in Bridges and houses, hedges and ditches; are an example of (PYQ-style)

  1. Assonance
  2. Consonance
  3. Simile
  4. Antithesis
Answer

Answer: (b) - consonant sounds are repeated inside nearby words.

4.7 Hyperbole ALSO ASKED Exaggeration and understatement HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningMaking something sound much bigger, more or worse than it is, on purpose. Say: hy-PUR-buh-lee (not "hyper-bowl").
Exam definitionHyperbole is deliberate, obvious exaggeration used for emphasis or humour; it is not meant to be taken literally.
Key / clue wordsa thousand times a ton of mountains of for ages a million in the wink of an eye dying of
How to spot it1. Is the statement impossible, or far beyond the truth? 2. Is the speaker exaggerating on purpose to stress a feeling? Yes + yes → hyperbole.
Memory trickHYPERbole goes over the top; LITOTES says "not bad" for "good". (A hyper child has too much energy; hyperbole has too much size.)

Easy examples

  • I have told you a thousand times to switch off the fan. a thousand times
  • This school bag weighs a ton! a ton
  • We waited for ages at the bus stop in the Chennai sun. for ages

From the syllabus poems

It seems to know mountains of information by heart.
My Computer Needs a Break · Shanthini Govindan
Hyperbole (also metaphor) — information cannot form mountains; the size is exaggerated to show how much the computer knows.
And ever again, in the wink of an eye, Painted stations whistle by.
From a Railway Carriage · Robert Louis Stevenson
Hyperbole — the stations seem to pass in a wink, to show the great speed of the train. (The whistling stations are personification and onomatopoeia.)

DO NOT CONFUSE: "As big as a mountain" has a comparing word (simile) AND an impossible size (hyperbole). If the question asks about the comparing word, answer simile (3.2); if it asks about exaggeration, answer hyperbole. Hyperbole says MORE than the truth; litotes says LESS (4.8).

Quick check

QC7. Identify the figure of speech: And inside you can tell it has a ton of space, (PYQ-style)

  1. Litotes
  2. Euphemism
  3. Metonymy
  4. Hyperbole
Answer

Answer: (d) - space cannot be weighed in tons; the size is exaggerated on purpose.

4.8 Litotes and Understatement ALSO ASKED Exaggeration and understatement

MeaningSaying less than you mean, often by using "not" with the opposite word ("not bad" = good). Say: LY-tuh-teez.
Exam definitionLitotes is understatement using a negative: an idea is expressed by denying its opposite ("not bad" = good; "unreluctant" = eager). Understatement (the wider term) makes something sound smaller or less serious than it really is.
Key / clue wordsnot bad not unkind no small not a simple un- / in- words (unreluctant) a bit
How to spot it1. Look for "not", "no" or "un-" before a word. 2. Remove the negative and take the opposite: "not bad" → good. 3. The real meaning is STRONGER than the words → litotes / understatement.
Memory trickHYPERbole goes over the top; LITOTES says "not bad" for "good". LITotes = LITtle words, big meaning.

Easy examples

  • Your idli is not bad at all! not bad = very good
  • Getting a state rank is no small achievement. no small = very big
  • On a 42-degree day in Chennai, Ravi said, "It is a bit warm today." understatement

From the syllabus poems

With forward face and unreluctant soul;
Life · Henry Van Dyke
Litotes — "unreluctant" (not unwilling) means eager and willing. (The f - f sound in the same line is alliteration, 3.1.)
Not a simple jab of the knife Will do it.
On Killing a Tree · Gieve Patel
Litotes / understatement — by denying the small action, the poet means that killing a tree needs far more cruel effort.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Litotes says LESS to mean MORE ("not bad" = good). Euphemism says something SOFTER to hide a harsh fact ("passed away" = died, 4.9). Litotes and hyperbole are opposites: one shrinks the truth, the other blows it up.

Quick check

QC8. Identify the figure of speech: Not so much pain will do it. (PYQ-style)

  1. Litotes
  2. Hyperbole
  3. Pun
  4. Oxymoron
Answer

Answer: (a) - a negative statement means that much more pain is needed to kill the tree.

4.9 Euphemism ALSO ASKED Exaggeration and understatement

MeaningA mild, polite word used in place of a harsh or unpleasant one. Say: YOO-fuh-miz-um.
Exam definitionEuphemism is the use of a mild or indirect word or phrase for something harsh, sad or unpleasant ("passed away" for "died").
Key / clue wordspassed away rest in peace sleep (= death) depart lie in the earth between jobs put to sleep
How to spot it1. Is the real topic unpleasant (death, illness, failure, old age, losing a job)? 2. Is a soft, polite phrase used instead of the blunt word? Yes + yes → euphemism.
Memory trickEUphemism = EU (good) word for a harsh fact. (Greek eu = good, pheme = speech.)

Easy examples

  • Our neighbour's grandfather passed away last night. passed away = died
  • My uncle is between jobs at the moment. = has no job
  • The old, sick dog was put to sleep. = killed painlessly

From the syllabus poems

The sands of life are nearly run, Let such in peace depart!
Your Space · David Bates
Euphemism — "depart" gently means "die". (The sands of life running out, as in an hourglass, is a metaphor.)
And quiet sleep and a sweet dream when the long trick's over.
Sea Fever · John Masefield
Euphemism (also metaphor) — a "trick" is a sailor's turn of duty at the wheel; the end of the long trick gently means the end of life.
Is earth like this, in which we all shall lie.
No Men are Foreign · James Falconer Kirkup
Euphemism — "lie" in the earth softly means to die and be buried.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Euphemism SOFTENS a harsh fact ("passed away"). Litotes UNDERSTATES with a negative to mean more ("not bad" = good, 4.8). Many euphemisms are also metaphors (sleep = death); if the question asks for "a mild way of saying something unpleasant", the answer is euphemism.

Quick check

QC9. The Fly says that those who visit the Spider's bed die, but puts it gently: They never, never wake again, who sleep upon your bed! Identify the figure of speech. (PYQ-style)

  1. Hyperbole
  2. Euphemism
  3. Onomatopoeia
  4. Chiasmus
Answer

Answer: (b) - "never wake again" is a mild way of saying "die".

4.10 Irony and Sarcasm ALSO ASKED Indirect meaning HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningIrony: the opposite of what is said or expected. Sarcasm: bitter irony used to hurt or mock someone.
Exam definitionIrony is the opposite of what is said or expected. Verbal irony: the words say the opposite of what is meant. Situational irony: what happens is the opposite of what is expected. Dramatic irony: the reader or audience knows something that a character does not. Sarcasm is bitter, mocking verbal irony meant to hurt.
Key / clue wordsOh, great! What lovely ...! (said about something bad) praise that is really blame an upside-down result the reader knows, the character does not
How to spot it1. Words say one thing, the meaning is the opposite → verbal irony (bitter and hurtful → sarcasm). 2. The result is the opposite of what we expect → situational irony. 3. We know a danger or truth that the character does not → dramatic irony.
Memory trickIRONY turns things upside down: Words (verbal), Events (situational), Knowledge (dramatic). SARCASM comes from a Greek word meaning "to tear flesh" — it is irony that bites.

Easy examples

  • Looking at a torn, dirty notebook, the teacher says, "What a beautiful notebook!" verbal irony; said to mock = sarcasm
  • A fire station burns down. situational irony
  • In a film, we see the thief hiding behind the door, but the hero walks in smiling. dramatic irony

From the syllabus poems

Says the ant to the cricket, "I'm your servant and friend, But we ants never borrow; we ants never lend.
The Ant and the Cricket · adapted from Aesop's fables
Verbal irony — the ant calls himself the cricket's servant and friend, then at once refuses to help him.
"You sang, Sir, you say? Go then," says the ant, "and dance the winter away."
The Ant and the Cricket · adapted from Aesop's fables
Sarcasm — the ant does not really want the starving cricket to dance; he mocks him bitterly for singing all summer.
Said the cunning Spider to the Fly, "Dear friend what can I do, To prove the warm affection I've always felt for you?
The Spider and the Fly · Mary Botham Howitt
Verbal irony + dramatic irony — the Spider's "warm affection" is really hunger; we readers know the Spider's plan while the Fly is being tempted.

Two more: the title of A Tragic Story is ironic, because the poem is a comic tale of a wise sage who acts foolishly; and On Killing a Tree uses a calm, step-by-step "how to kill" tone that exposes the cruelty (see 5.25 and 5.9).

DO NOT CONFUSE: All sarcasm is irony, but not all irony is sarcasm. Sarcasm is aimed at a PERSON, to hurt; a fire station burning down is irony but not sarcasm. Plain bad luck (rain on a picnic day) is NOT irony unless the result is the exact opposite of what was expected or planned.

Quick check

  1. Simile
  2. Apostrophe
  3. Alliteration
  4. Irony

Answer: (d) - the title says the opposite of what the poem really is.

4.11 Paradox ALSO ASKED Contrast

MeaningA statement that sounds impossible or self-contradictory, but has a truth inside it.
Exam definitionParadox is a statement that seems self-contradictory (or absurd) but contains a truth.
Key / clue wordsyet but the more ... the less less is more a whole sentence that sounds impossible
How to spot it1. First reading: "This is impossible!" 2. Second thought: "But it is true in a deeper way." 3. It is a whole statement, not just two words (two clashing words = oxymoron, 4.3).
Memory trickA PARADOX is a puzzle sentence: it sounds FALSE, then turns out TRUE.

Easy examples

  • "The child is father of the man." (Wordsworth) childhood shapes the adult
  • More haste, less speed. rushing slows you down
  • "I must be cruel, only to be kind." (Shakespeare) cruel to be kind

From the syllabus poems

Seeming still, yet still in motion,
The River · Caroline Ann Bowles
Paradox — the broad river looks still (calm), yet it is always moving towards the ocean, like a person in the prime of life. Note the play on "still": first "calm", then "even now".

DO NOT CONFUSE: Paradox = a whole STATEMENT that seems impossible but is true ("The child is father of the man"). Oxymoron = two clashing WORDS side by side ("deafening silence", 4.3). Antithesis = opposite ideas balanced, with no contradiction ("To err is human, to forgive divine", 4.12).

Quick check

  1. Onomatopoeia
  2. Metonymy
  3. Paradox
  4. Climax
Answer

4.12 Antithesis ALSO ASKED Contrast HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningOpposite ideas placed side by side in balanced words or clauses, to make the contrast sharp.
Exam definitionAntithesis is the placing of opposite (contrasting) ideas side by side in balanced words, phrases or clauses.
Key / clue wordsOpposite pairs: friend / foe love / fear lost / won victory / defeat rich / poor err / forgive; balance words: not ... but or than
How to spot it1. Find two opposite words or ideas. 2. Check the balance: the two halves have a similar pattern. 3. Both halves are true, with no contradiction → antithesis.
Memory trickANTI = against: ANTI-thesis puts an idea against its opposite, like the two pans of a weighing scale.

Easy examples

  • "To err is human, to forgive divine." (Pope) err / forgive; human / divine
  • Speech is silver, silence is golden. speech / silence
  • United we stand, divided we fall. united / divided; stand / fall

From the syllabus poems

I was angry with my friend; I told my wrath, my wrath did end. I was angry with my foe: I told it not, my wrath did grow.
A Poison Tree · William Blake
Antithesis — friend / foe, told / told not, end / grow are set against each other in two balanced pairs of lines.
Speak gently! - It is better far To rule by love, than fear
Your Space · David Bates
Antithesis — ruling by love is set against ruling by fear.
It's written in victory and defeat And every trial a man may meet.
Courage · Edgar Albert Guest
Antithesis — victory and defeat, two opposites, are placed together.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Antithesis = two opposite IDEAS in balanced parts, both true. Oxymoron = two clashing WORDS joined into one phrase ("empty joy", 4.3). Paradox = one statement that SEEMS impossible (4.11). Chiasmus = the same words repeated in reverse order (4.24).

Quick check

QC12. Identify the figure of speech: For the battle is lost or the battle is won (PYQ-style)

  1. Antithesis
  2. Oxymoron
  3. Simile
  4. Euphemism
Answer

Answer: (a) - the opposites "lost" and "won" are placed in balanced phrases.

4.13 Epigram ALSO ASKED Contrast

MeaningA short, clever and memorable saying, often with a twist or a contrast.
Exam definitionAn epigram is a short, witty, memorable saying that states a truth in a neat way, often with a surprising twist or contrast.
Key / clue wordsshort witty easy to quote a general truth in one line a twist at the end
How to spot it1. Is it short (usually one line)? 2. Is it clever and easy to quote? 3. Does it state a general truth in a neat, balanced way? Yes → epigram.
Memory trickEPIGRAM = a line fit for the school notice board: short, smart, and it sticks in the mind.

Easy examples

  • "Brevity is the soul of wit." (Shakespeare) short and witty
  • "Little strokes fell great oaks." (Benjamin Franklin) small efforts, big results
  • "I can resist everything except temptation." (Oscar Wilde) witty twist

From the syllabus poems

A thing of beauty is a joy for ever:
A Thing of Beauty · John Keats
Epigram — the famous opening line states a general truth in a few memorable words.
"Persistence is the key to everything,"
I am Every Woman · Rakhi Nariani Shirke
Epigram (also metaphor) — a short, quotable saying: persistence is called a "key" that opens every door.

DO NOT CONFUSE: EpiGRAM (a witty saying) ≠ EpiGRAPH (a quotation printed at the start of a book or chapter) ≠ EpiTAPH (words written on a tomb). A proverb is an old saying of the people with no known author; an epigram is usually the work of a known writer. An epigram often contains antithesis or paradox: "To err is human, to forgive divine" (Pope) is both an epigram and an antithesis.

Quick check

  2. Epigram
  3. Apostrophe
  4. Ellipsis
Answer

4.14 Pun ALSO ASKED Indirect meaning

MeaningA play on words: one word (or two words that sound alike) used with two meanings at once, usually for fun.
Exam definitionA pun is a play on two meanings of a word, or on words that sound alike, for a humorous or clever effect.
Key / clue wordsWords with two meanings: cricket virus worm mouse bank weak / week two-tired / too tired
How to spot it1. Find a word that fits two meanings at the same time. 2. Replace it with a synonym: if the joke disappears, it was a pun.
Memory trickA PUN is FUN: one word, two meanings, one smile.

Easy examples

  • A bicycle cannot stand on its own because it is two-tired. two-tired / too tired
  • Why is a river rich? Because it has two banks. bank = river side / money bank
  • Seven days without a walk makes one weak. weak / week

From the syllabus poems

Folks call this a fable. I'll warrant it true: Some crickets have four legs, and some have two.
The Ant and the Cricket · adapted from Aesop's fables
Pun — "cricket" means the insect (four legs, as the poet counts them) and also a lazy, carefree person (two legs) who plays all summer and saves nothing.

Pun — a computer worm (a harmful program) and an earthworm that is gobbled up.
Then my computer caught a virus, and fell very sick,
My Computer Needs a Break · Shanthini Govindan
Pun + Personification — a computer virus and a germ that makes people ill; the computer "falls sick" like a person.

DO NOT CONFUSE: A pun needs TWO meanings working at once. A word used in only one meaning ("The bat flew out of the cave") is not a pun. When a line has both a pun and personification (the sick computer), answer what the question points to: the double-meaning word → pun; the human action → personification (3.4).

4.15 Metonymy ALSO ASKED Association and reference HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningCalling something by the name of a thing closely connected with it. Say: mi-TON-uh-mee.
Exam definitionMetonymy is naming something by an associated thing (uniforms for soldiers; the crown for the king; the pen for writing).
Key / clue wordsthe crown (king, queen) the pen (writing) the sword (war, force) the bench (judges) the press (newspapers) the stage (acting) uniforms (soldiers)
How to spot it1. The word is NOT a part of the real thing, only something linked with it (a tool, symbol, place or dress). 2. Replace it with the real meaning: "the pen" → writing. Linked thing → metonymy; a part of the thing → synecdoche (4.16).
Memory trickSynecdoche = Some part for the whole; Metonymy = Mention a related thing.

Easy examples

  • The bench gave its judgement today. bench = judges
  • Have you read Bharathiyar? the poet's name for his poems
  • Kodambakkam has released three new films this week. a place for the Tamil film industry

From the syllabus poems

Beneath all uniforms, a single body breathes
No Men are Foreign · James Falconer Kirkup
Metonymy — "uniforms" stands for soldiers. A uniform is not a part of a soldier's body, only connected with him. (The full sentence, which compares their bodies with ours using "like", is a simile — see 3.2.)

DO NOT CONFUSE: "All hands on deck" — hands are a PART of the sailors → synecdoche (4.16). "Uniforms" are not a part of the soldiers, only CONNECTED with them → metonymy. Many books treat synecdoche as a special kind of metonymy; if both are options, ask: "part or connection?"

Quick check

  1. Synecdoche
  2. Pun
  3. Oxymoron
  4. Metonymy

4.16 Synecdoche ALSO ASKED Association and reference HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningA part used for the whole, or the whole used for a part. Say: si-NEK-duh-kee.
Exam definitionSynecdoche is a figure in which a part stands for the whole, or the whole stands for a part (hands for workers; soul for person; roof for home).
Key / clue wordshands (workers) heads (people, cattle) mouths to feed soul (person) roof (home) wheels (vehicle) India won (the team)
How to spot it1. Is the word a PART of the real thing (the hands of a worker, the roof of a home)? 2. Or is the WHOLE used for a part ("India won" = the Indian team)? Either → synecdoche.
Memory trickSynecdoche = Some part for the whole; Metonymy = Mention a related thing.

Easy examples

  • All hands on deck! hands = sailors
  • The farmer has five mouths to feed. mouths = family members
  • India won the match by six wickets. whole country = the team

From the syllabus poems

Their hands are ours, and in their lines we read
No Men are Foreign · James Falconer Kirkup
Synecdoche — "hands" (a part) stands for the working people of other lands; their labour is like ours.
It's the old home roof that shelters all the charm that life can give;
The Stick-together Families · Edgar Albert Guest
Synecdoche — the roof (a part) stands for the whole home and family.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Synecdoche uses a PART (hands, roof, soul); metonymy uses a CONNECTED thing (uniforms, crown, pen, 4.15). A quick test: is it a part of the thing itself? Hands are part of the worker's body → synecdoche. A crown or a uniform can be taken off; it is only connected with the person → metonymy.

4.17 Transferred Epithet ALSO ASKED Association and reference HIGH-FREQUENCY

MeaningAn adjective that really describes a person is moved to a thing, a time or an action connected with that person.
Exam definitionA transferred epithet is an adjective (epithet) transferred from the person it really describes to a thing connected with that person ("busy day", "sleepless night").
Key / clue wordsFeeling or state adjective + a thing or time: sleepless night busy day weary way anxious hours nervous wait lazy afternoon
How to spot it1. Find an adjective + noun pair. 2. Ask: "Can this NOUN really feel or be this?" (Can a night be sleepless? No — the person is.) 3. The quality belongs to a person nearby → transferred epithet.
Memory trickTransferred epithet: the night was not sleepless - YOU were.

Easy examples

  • I spent a sleepless night before the exam. I was sleepless, not the night
  • After a busy day, Amma made hot idlis for us. Amma was busy, not the day
  • "The ploughman homeward plods his weary way." (Thomas Gray) the ploughman is weary, not the way

From the syllabus poems

And the finest of conventions ever held beneath the sun Are the little family gatherings when the busy day is done.
The Stick-together Families · Edgar Albert Guest
Transferred epithet — the people are busy, not the day; the adjective has moved from the people to the day.

DO NOT CONFUSE: "The angry sea" — the sea itself is shown raging like a person → personification (3.4). "A sleepless night" — the quality really belongs to a nearby PERSON → transferred epithet. Always ask: "Whose quality is it really?"

Quick check

QC17. Identify the figure of speech in the phrase "busy day": But the gladdest sort of people, when the busy day is done, (PYQ-style)

  1. Oxymoron
  2. Personification
  3. Transferred epithet
  4. Hyperbole

Answer: (c) - the people are busy; the adjective is transferred to the day.

4.18 Rhetorical Question ALSO ASKED Indirect meaning

MeaningA question asked for effect, not to get an answer.
Exam definitionA rhetorical question is a question asked for effect or emphasis, not for an answer; the answer is obvious, or no answer is expected.
Key / clue words? (no reply follows) How could ...? Who knows ...? Isn't it ...? What will become of ...?
How to spot it1. Find the question mark. 2. Does anyone answer it? Is an answer really needed? 3. If the question is really a strong statement or feeling → rhetorical question.
Memory trickA rhetorical question is ASKED, not ANSWERED — its answer is hidden inside it.

Easy examples

  • Who does not love Marina Beach at sunset? = everyone loves it
  • Do you think money grows on trees? = it does not
  • How many times must I tell you? no answer wanted

From the syllabus poems

"Oh! what will become," says cricket, "of me?"
The Ant and the Cricket · adapted from Aesop's fables
Rhetorical question (with exclamation) — the cricket expects no answer; the question shows his fear and despair.

Special Hero · Christina M. Kerschen
Rhetorical question — the child does not want an answer; the question shows wonder and thanks.
How could this be?
The House on Elm Street · Nadia Bush
Rhetorical question — the poet wonders at the tree that never grows leaves; no answer is given, and the next line admits the poet does not know.

DO NOT CONFUSE: "What time is it?" needs an answer → an ordinary question, not rhetorical. The cricket's cry above has two figures: "Oh!" is an exclamation (4.19), and the question that follows, which expects no answer, is a rhetorical question.

4.19 Exclamation ALSO ASKED Indirect meaning

MeaningA sudden cry of strong feeling — joy, sorrow, surprise, anger or wonder.
Exam definitionExclamation is a figure of speech in which strong feeling is expressed suddenly, usually with words like "Oh!", "Alas!", "Ah!", "What a ...!", "How ...!" and the exclamation mark (!).
Key / clue wordsOh! Alas! Ah! Hurrah! What a ...! How ...! !
How to spot it1. Look for "!" and words like Oh, Alas, Ah. 2. Is a strong feeling being poured out? Yes → exclamation. 3. If the line also speaks TO someone absent or to a thing, it is apostrophe too (4.1).
Memory trickEXCLAMATION = EXCLAIM = cry out a feeling: Alas = sad, Hurrah = glad.

Easy examples

  • Hurrah! We won the match! joy

  • What a beautiful sunrise at Kanyakumari! wonder

A Tragic Story · William Makepeace Thackeray

Exclamation — "Oh" and "!" make an earnest cry: win back, with gentle words, those who have gone wrong.

4.20 Inversion ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningChanging the normal order of words in a sentence, for rhyme, rhythm or emphasis.
Exam definitionInversion (also called anastrophe) is the reversal of the normal order of words in a sentence: Whose woods these are I think I know. for "I think I know whose woods these are."
Key / clue wordsverb before subject (Strong is she) adjective after noun (apple bright) object first Down came ... Never have I ...
How to spot it1. Read the line as normal prose — does it sound upside down? 2. Rewrite it in normal order (subject + verb + object). 3. Same meaning, only a different order → inversion.
Memory trickINVERSION = INVERTED (upside-down) word order. Put it back the right way up to check.

Easy examples

  • Sweet is the mango of Salem. = The mango of Salem is sweet.
  • Down came the rain. = The rain came down.
  • Never have I seen such a crowd at Marina Beach. = I have never seen ...

From the syllabus poems

Whose woods these are I think I know.
Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening · Robert Frost
Inversion — normal order: I think I know whose woods these are. (This line is NOT alliteration: "whose" begins with an h sound, "woods" with a w sound.)
In the morning glad I see, My foe outstretched beneath the tree.
A Poison Tree · William Blake
Inversion — normal order: In the morning I gladly see my foe lying dead under the tree.
That for themselves a cooling covert make
A Thing of Beauty · John Keats
Inversion — about the clear streams (rills); normal order: that make a cool, shady shelter for themselves.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Inversion changes the word order inside ONE sentence. Chiasmus repeats words in REVERSED order across TWO balanced parts (4.24). An adjective placed after its noun (an apple bright) is also inversion, but for that line of A Poison Tree the figure usually asked is allusion (4.2).

  4. Inversion

4.21 Epistrophe ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningThe same word or words repeated at the END of lines, clauses or sentences that come one after another. Say: i-PIS-truh-fee.
Exam definitionEpistrophe is the repetition of the same word(s) at the end of successive lines, clauses or sentences. It is the opposite of anaphora.
Key / clue wordsthe same ending words ... the people / ... the people ... no evil / ... no evil behind him
How to spot it1. Look at the LAST words of each line or clause. 2. The same word(s) at the end again and again → epistrophe. 3. The same words at the START → anaphora (3.6).
Memory trickANAphora is At the stArt; EPIstrophe is at the End.

Easy examples

  • "... government of the people, by the people, for the people ..." (Abraham Lincoln) the people
  • See no evil, hear no evil, speak no evil. no evil
  • In the morning it rained, in the afternoon it rained, all night it rained. it rained

From the syllabus poems

There lived a sage in days of yore, And he a handsome pigtail wore; But wondered much and sorrowed more, Because it hung behind him.
A Tragic Story · William Makepeace Thackeray
Epistrophe / refrain — every stanza of the poem closes with the same words, "behind him".

The same ending returns in the other stanzas: Not dangling there behind him. · The pigtail hung behind him. · Hung steadily behind him. Each time, the sage's spinning ends where it began — that is the joke of the poem.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Epistrophe = same words at the END (4.21). Anaphora = same words at the START (3.6). Refrain = a line or phrase that returns at regular intervals, usually at stanza ends (4.22). Rhyme = only the SOUND matches (sing - spring); in epistrophe the same WORD comes back.

4.22 Refrain ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningA line or phrase that comes back at regular places in a poem or song, usually at the end of each stanza — like a chorus.
Exam definitionA refrain is a line or phrase repeated at regular intervals in a poem or song, usually at the end of stanzas (a chorus).
Key / clue wordschorus the same last line in every stanza the pallavi of a song regular intervals
How to spot it1. Look at the end of each stanza, or at the chorus. 2. Does the same line or phrase come back at REGULAR places? Yes → refrain. 3. A line said just twice, side by side, is repetition, not refrain (3.7).
Memory trickREFRAIN = the line that RETURNS again and again, like the pallavi of a Tamil film song.

Easy examples

  • In "Twinkle, twinkle, little star", the opening lines come back at the end of the rhyme. refrain
  • "Old MacDonald had a farm, E-I-E-I-O" — "E-I-E-I-O" returns in every verse. refrain
  • The pallavi of a film song is sung again after every charanam. refrain

From the syllabus poems

Says he, "The mystery I've found, I'll turn me round," he turned him round; But still it hung behind him.
A Tragic Story · William Makepeace Thackeray
Refrain / epistrophe (also repetition) — the words of the last line change a little, but "behind him" closes all six stanzas, coming back at regular places like a chorus.
Lessons in life aren't always so simple
Lessons in Life · Brigette Bryant and Daniel Ho
Refrain (chorus) — this line opens the chorus of the song-poem; a chorus is sung again after each verse, so it works as a refrain.

DO NOT CONFUSE: And miles to go before I sleep, / And miles to go before I sleep. — the same line said twice, side by side, at the end of the poem = REPETITION (3.7), not refrain and not anaphora. A refrain returns at REGULAR intervals (every stanza or every chorus).

  3. Refrain
  4. Climax

4.23 Polysyndeton and Asyndeton ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningPolysyndeton: many joining words ("and ... and ... and") one after another. Asyndeton: the joining words are left out; only commas remain.
Exam definitionPolysyndeton is the use of many conjunctions (usually "and" or "or") in close succession. Asyndeton is the deliberate leaving out of conjunctions between words, phrases or clauses.
Key / clue wordsand ... and ... and nor ... nor (polysyndeton) commas only, no "and" (asyndeton)
How to spot it1. Count the "and"s: more than grammar needs → polysyndeton (it slows the line and piles things up). 2. A list joined only by commas, with no "and" before the last item → asyndeton (it speeds the line up and sounds urgent).
Memory trickPOLY = many (a polygon has many sides): many ANDs. A- = without (as in "atypical"): WITHOUT ands.

Easy examples

  • At the wedding we ate idli and dosa and vada and pongal. polysyndeton
  • "I came, I saw, I conquered." (Julius Caesar) asyndeton: no "and"
  • He ran, jumped, caught the ball. asyndeton

From the syllabus poems

And the wheel's kick and the wind's song and the white sail's shaking,
Sea Fever · John Masefield
Polysyndeton — "and" joins every item; the sights and sounds of the ship pile up one after another.
Louder, faster, brawling, leaping,
The River · Caroline Ann Bowles
Asyndeton — no "and" at all; the quick list matches the rushing, impatient river of youth.
Browning, hardening, Twisting, withering,
On Killing a Tree · Gieve Patel
Asyndeton — the stages of the tree's death follow one another with only commas.

4.24 Chiasmus ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningA criss-cross pattern: words from the first half come back in reverse order in the second half (A-B, B-A). Say: ky-AZ-mus.
Exam definitionChiasmus is a criss-cross (ABBA) reversal of words or ideas in two parallel clauses.
Key / clue wordsA ... B / B ... A eat to live / live to eat ask not ... ask fair ... foul / foul ... fair
How to spot it1. Split the sentence into two halves. 2. Label the key words of the first half A and B. 3. If the second half has them as B and A → chiasmus.
Memory trickCHIasmus comes from the Greek letter CHI, written X: the words cross over like an X.

Easy examples

  • "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." (John F. Kennedy) country - you / you - country
  • When the going gets tough, the tough get going. going - tough / tough - going
  • "Fair is foul, and foul is fair." (Shakespeare) fair - foul / foul - fair

From the syllabus poems

Remember, no men are strange, no countries foreign
No Men are Foreign · James Falconer Kirkup
Chiasmus — the first line; the last line reverses the pair: Remember, no men are foreign, and no countries strange. Strange - foreign (A-B) becomes foreign - strange (B-A), so the poem ends where it began.

DO NOT CONFUSE: Antithesis needs OPPOSITE ideas (4.12); chiasmus needs the SAME words in REVERSED order. "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" has both. Inversion upsets the word order inside ONE clause (4.20); chiasmus mirrors TWO clauses.

4.25 Climax and Anticlimax ALSO ASKED Repetition and order

MeaningClimax: words or ideas arranged in rising order of importance, like steps going up. Anticlimax: a sudden drop from something important to something small or silly.
Exam definitionClimax is the arrangement of words, phrases or ideas in rising (ascending) order of importance or force. Anticlimax (also called bathos) is a sudden fall from something serious or important to something trivial, often for humour.
Key / clue wordsa list of three or more small → bigger → biggest first ... then ... finally a silly last item (anticlimax)
How to spot it1. Check the order of a list of three or more items. 2. Each step bigger or stronger than the last → climax. 3. Big, big, big ... then small and silly → anticlimax.
Memory trickCLIMAX comes from the Greek word for "ladder": climb up, step by step. ANTI-climax = slipping off the ladder at the last step.

From the syllabus poems

4.27 Extended Metaphor and Allegory ALSO ASKED Comparison

MeaningExtended metaphor: one comparison carried on through many lines or a whole poem. Allegory: a whole story with a hidden moral meaning; a fable is a short allegory, often with talking animals.
Exam definitionAn extended metaphor is a metaphor developed over several lines or a whole poem. An allegory is a whole story whose characters and events stand for a hidden moral meaning; a fable is a short allegory, often with animals that talk, ending in a moral.

6 Do Not Confuse: High-Yield Pairs

Many wrong answers come from mixing up two look-alike figures. This section puts the 14 most-confused groups side by side. 6.1 Simile vs Metaphor HIGH-FREQUENCY

BasisSimile (3.2)Metaphor (3.3)
MeaningAn open comparison of two UNLIKE things using a comparing word.A hidden comparison: one thing is said to BE another thing, with no comparing word.
Clue wordslike as as ... as so ... as as if as though thanNo comparing word. Often is are was joins two unlike things, or one word is used in a non-literal sense.
TestPoint to the comparing word, then check that it joins two UNLIKE things. Both yes = simile.Put "like" in the middle. If it still makes sense as a comparison, the original is a metaphor.
StrengthSofter: A is LIKE B - the two stay separate.Stronger: A IS B - the two become one.
Easy exampleChennai in May is as hot as an oven.Chennai in May is an oven.
From the poemsLike a child at play. (The River); And charging along like troops in a battle, (From a Railway Carriage)She's a lioness; don't mess with her. (I am Every Woman); the journey will be joy (Life)
Your eyes are like the diamond bright, but mine are dull as lead!
The Spider and the Fly · Mary Botham Howitt
Simile — two comparing words in one line: eyes like diamonds, dull as lead. Drop the comparing word ("Your eyes are diamonds") and it turns into a Metaphor.

MEMORY TRICK: Simile Says "like"; Metaphor Means "is" - same picture, different grammar.

6.2 Metaphor vs Personification

BasisMetaphor (3.3)Personification (3.4)

TestAsk: "A is turned INTO what?" If A is simply called something it is not (an oven, a lioness, a hero), it is a metaphor.Ask: "Is a non-human doing what only PEOPLE do?" If yes, it is personification.

Personification (line 1) — a machine is called brainy and smart, like a clever student. Metaphor (line 2) — a huge store of facts is called mountains (also hyperbole).

6.14 False Similes: when "like" or "as" is not a simile

A true simile needs three things: (1) a comparing word, (2) two UNLIKE things, (3) an imaginative point. "Like" and "as" also do ordinary jobs - as a verb, as "when", as "such as", and in plain measurement - so check all three before you answer Simile.

SentenceSimile? (Yes / No)Why

7.5 When one line has many figures

Good poetry often packs several figures into one line. List them all, then choose the one the question points to.

Faster than fairies, faster than witches,
From a Railway Carriage · Robert Louis Stevenson
Present: Alliteration (the f sound in Faster, fairies, faster, 3.1); Simile (the speed is compared with fairies and witches using than; textbook keys count this as a simile, 3.2); Hyperbole (no train is really faster than fairies, 4.7); Repetition (faster than comes twice, 3.7). Choose: ALLITERATION is the safest single answer. If Alliteration is not offered, choose Simile. Choose Hyperbole only when the question asks about exaggeration.
River, river, little river! Bright you sparkle on your way;
The River · Caroline Ann Bowles
Present: Apostrophe (the poet speaks directly TO the river and calls it "you", as if it could hear, 4.1); Personification (the river is treated as a living being going on its way, 3.4); Repetition ("river" three times in line 1, 3.7). Choose: if an option reads "Apostrophe and Personification", take it. For the address in line 1, choose APOSTROPHE. If Apostrophe is not offered, choose PERSONIFICATION. Choose Repetition only when the question asks about the repeated word.
We can pull and haul and push and lift and drive,
The Secret of the Machines · Rudyard Kipling
Present: Polysyndeton (and ... and ... and ... and, 4.23); Anaphora (the line opens with We can, and so do the next three lines, such as We can print and plough and weave and heat and light,, 3.6); Personification (the machines speak as "We", 3.4); a weak p-alliteration (pull, push) that is not the one to choose. Choose: ANAPHORA when two or more We can lines are quoted; POLYSYNDETON when only this line is quoted and it is offered, or when the question points to the many "and"s; PERSONIFICATION when the question asks how the machines are presented.
To the gull's way and the whale's way where the wind's like a whetted knife;
Sea Fever · John Masefield
Present: Simile (the sharp, cutting wind is compared with a whetted, that is sharpened, knife using like, 3.2); Alliteration (the w sound in way, whale's, way, where, wind's, whetted, 3.1); Repetition ("way" twice, 3.7); Imagery (you can feel the wind cut, 4.26). Choose: SIMILE when the question is about the wind or the comparison; ALLITERATION when it is about sound or the w-words. If the whole line is quoted, both are offered and the stem gives no hint, choose SIMILE: "like" comparing two unlike things makes it certain. (In the first card the only comparing word was "than", so Alliteration was safer there.)

EXAM TIP: Underline the words the question quotes or points to; the figure it wants lives in those words. When two answers still fit, prefer the one that is beyond doubt (a clear like or as, an O-address, a sound-word, a plain f-f-f) over one you would have to argue for.

7.6 Exam-day checklist

  1. Read the stem twice. Is it "identify the figure", "rhyming words", "rhyme scheme", "match", or "which is NOT"?
  2. Read the quoted line aloud in your mind: sound figures need the ear, not the eye.
  3. Circle the clue words: like, as, than, is, O, !, ?, and ... and, not (7.3).
  4. Run the 10-Second Method (7.2) and tick every figure that fits.
  5. Match your ticks with the options; choose what the question points to (7.5).
  6. Watch the traps: "like" or "as" without an unlike comparison is not a simile; the same LETTER is not always the same SOUND.
  7. For a rhyme scheme, write the last word of each line first, then the letters: same end-sound = same letter, new end-sound = new letter.
  8. Place the line in its poem (1.2). Knowing the poem's usual figures (Section 5) confirms your answer.

9.4 The 10-Second Method: Recap

Ask these ten questions in this order, as in 7.2. Tick every "yes", then match your ticks with the options; when two or more fit, answer the one the question points to (7.5).

  1. Sound-word? buzz, click, whistle, snap → Onomatopoeia.
  2. Same first sound in nearby words? Say it aloud → Alliteration. Same vowel sound → Assonance; same consonant inside or at the end → Consonance.
  3. like / as / as ... as / than joining two UNLIKE things → Simile.
  4. A is B, with no like / as → Metaphor; kept up over many lines → Extended metaphor.
  5. Thing acting human (thinks, smiles, sits, speaks) → Personification.
  6. Addressing the absent — a dead or absent person, a thing or an idea called "you" or "O ..." → Apostrophe.
  7. Repeated starts or ends? Same start → Anaphora; same end → Epistrophe; a line back after every stanza → Refrain; the same word or line anywhere → Repetition.
  8. Opposites? Two words side by side → Oxymoron; balanced opposite ideas → Antithesis; a whole statement that seems false but is true → Paradox.
  9. Exaggeration or understatement? Over the top → Hyperbole; "not bad" for "good" → Litotes; a soft word for a harsh fact → Euphemism.
  10. Reference or omission? A famous person or story → Allusion; words left out that you can supply → Ellipsis.

9.5 Ten Golden Rules

  1. Alliteration is sound, not letter. keep the courage of the quest repeats the k sound, so it is alliteration; Whose woods is h + w, so it is NOT.
  2. like / as must compare two UNLIKE things. "I like mangoes" and "She is as tall as her brother" are not similes. A "than" comparison also counts: Faster than a cheetah = simile.
  3. Anaphora = same START of lines. We were taken … We were melted = anaphora. A whole line said twice (And miles to go before I sleep) = repetition. Same END = epistrophe; a line after every stanza = refrain.
  4. Apostrophe needs the absent or a thing. River, river, little river! = apostrophe (+ personification). Talking to a doctor who is in the room is NOT apostrophe.
  5. Acting human = personification; spoken to = apostrophe. Talking animals in fables (the spider, the fly, the ant, the cricket) = personification.
  6. Check what the question highlights. One line can hold two or three figures; the underlined or bold word decides. For Faster than fairies, faster than witches, alliteration is the safest single answer.
  7. Free verse has no rhyme scheme. No Men are Foreign, On Killing a Tree and Making Life Worth While are free verse. I am Every Woman and The House on Elm Street have scattered rhymes but no regular scheme.
  8. New sound = new letter. A sound used before takes its old letter back: Stopping by Woods = aaba bbcb ccdc dddd.
  9. Rhyme by ear, not by eye. Near rhymes such as arms - harm, smooth - youth and scheme - team are accepted in textbook answers.
  10. Oxymoron = two words; paradox = a whole statement. empty joy = oxymoron; Seeming still, yet still in motion = paradox.

9.6 Last Word Before the Exam
